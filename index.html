Anticipated Coercion, Undetected Effects

Sanctions, capital flow volatility and payment-system diversification in the Global South. An interactive companion to the paper.
Weaponised interdependence theory predicts that states exposed to financial chokepoints, such as the SWIFT messaging system, should show more volatile capital flows when sanctioned. I test this on a country-year panel (about 150 countries) and find no statistically significant effect in either specification. I argue the null is compatible with states adapting before sanctions bite, and use India's UPI corridors as a descriptive illustration of that kind of hedging.

Three questions

H1. Do sanctioned states show higher capital flow volatility?
Estimate 0.122, p = 0.56. Not distinguishable from zero.
H2. Does capital account openness change that?
Interaction 0.007, p = 0.96. No evidence it does.
H3 (post hoc). Could pre-emptive adaptation hide the effect?
This is an interpretation that the panel cannot test directly. It is a hypothesis for future work, not a result.

Outcome: 3-year rolling standard deviation of the net financial account (% of GDP). Method: two-way fixed effects with country-clustered standard errors. Use the tabs above to explore the data.

What a null can and cannot say: it shows no detectable average effect in this design. It does not show that sanctions have no effect, and the sample may mix very different kinds of cases.

What happens around the first sanction?

If states hedge before sanctions arrive, volatility should already look different in the years leading up to onset. This plot tracks volatility from five years before to five years after a country is first sanctioned, compared with the year before (k = −1, set to zero).

Hover or tap a point.
Reading it honestly. No pre-onset coefficient is statistically significant, and no post-onset coefficient is either. That rules out a visible pre-trend, but it does not confirm that states adapted in advance. The intervals are wide, so the data cannot separate "no effect" from "moderate effects we cannot detect". Anticipatory adaptation stays a hypothesis here.

Countries first sanctioned in 1975 or later (earlier ones are already treated and are dropped); never-sanctioned countries are the comparison group. Country and year fixed effects, controls as in the main model, SEs clustered by country. End points (−5, +5) are binned, and intervals running past the axis are clipped (k = −4 is far wider than shown). Because volatility is a 3-year rolling SD, neighbouring coefficients are mechanically related. With staggered timing, two-way fixed effects estimates can be biased, so treat this as a diagnostic.

India's UPI corridors

A separate, descriptive exercise that is not part of the regression panel. Tap a country for its centrality. Use the dropdown to see how the picture changes if the corridor weights are coded differently.

Tap a node.

Weights are my own coding of how deep each corridor is, so they are a judgement call. Centrality is computed on the undirected weighted projection. There are no direct links between any two partner countries, so the network is a pure star.

Static figure from the repo

Stress test: what if a corridor goes away?

Switch corridors off and see how India's total corridor weight and each partner's share change. This is a sensitivity exercise on my coded weights, not a forecast.

In a star network the hub always has eigenvector centrality of 1/√2, and each partner's score is just its weight relative to the others, so removing a spoke mechanically raises the others' scores. That is why the page reports total corridor weight and share of India's weight, which are easier to interpret. A real resilience analysis would need links among partners and actual transaction volumes, which I do not have.
